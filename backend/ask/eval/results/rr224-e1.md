> Objective
> The 2026-01-20 dashboard review found setups of Compass AI for a practice stopping most at Connect calendar (7 of 24 setups started) and then at Invite your team (3 of 24), and said a dashboard can't show why ( 2026-01-20-dashboard-review-compass-ai-practice-setup-completion/session-notes.md:45-46,72 ). > Method

> Researcher: Priya Patel (moderator)

> Build: Steps 3 and 4 are required in this build. There is no "Connect later" and no "Skip for now"; the only way past each step is to complete it.

> Test accommodation: When a participant stopped at step 3, the moderator told them it was the sandbox calendar and let them continue. Every participant completed step 3 this way or on their own, so completing step 3 in this session is not a result.
> Not covered: The step 4 screen doesn't say when the invite email is sent, and these notes don't record when this build sends it. How Compass AI access is provisioned (through SSO groups or through these invites) wasn't known to the moderator and isn't recorded in the corpus.
> Key Findings

All 4 continued only on the sandbox calendar (see Test accommodation).
> [MEDIUM] (calendar: what is read) Both IT Administrators (P158, P159) connected the calendar, but only after asking which fields are read (for example, whether visit reasons are included). The screen doesn't list them; the moderator couldn't answer, and both went on without an answer.
> [HIGH] (invite: timing and training) 3 of 4 Practice Administrators (P154, P156, P157) hesitated at Invite your team. They weren't sure whether the email goes out when they finish the step or at Review & finish, and they didn't want staff emailed before champion training is booked for the practice. P155 entered the test staff list and went on without comment.
> [MEDIUM] (invite: provisioning) One IT Administrator (P159) asked whether the invites create accounts or rely on the SSO groups staff are already in. The moderator couldn't answer, and the corpus doesn't record how access is provisioned.
> [LOW] (sign in and practice details) No issues at Sign in or Practice details: 6 of 6 participants completed both without help or comment on a problem.

**[1] Usability Test — Ambient Scribe Sound-Alike Medication Flag Concept** · `raw:2026-02-10-ambient-scribe-medication-flag-concept`

> Objective
> The GA-candidate round (2026-01-13) recommended treating sound-alike medication errors as an ongoing post-GA monitoring item rather than a launch blocker, since the existing confidence-highlighted variant already surfaces them as generically low-confidence. This session tests an early concept that goes one step further: a distinct flag specifically for suspected sound-alike medication names, visually separate from the general low-confidence underline, to see whether participants notice and act on it differently than they do on ordinary low-confidence text.
> Method
> Method: Moderated usability test, 6 simulated encounters seeded with known sound-alike pairs (e.g. hydralazine/hydroxyzine, clonidine/Klonopin), 40 min/session
> Researcher: Priya Patel
> Full participant roster: see participants.md in this folder
> Key Findings
> [HIGH] (flag recognized correctly) 5 of 6 participants correctly identified the medication-specific flag (a distinct amber pill-shaped badge, vs. the generic low-confidence underline) as "different from the usual low-confidence thing" within the first two encounters, without being told the distinction existed.
> [MEDIUM] (over-trust risk) 2 participants said the badge's specificity made them more likely to treat unflagged medication lines as automatically correct — the opposite of the intended effect, and worth watching as adoption scales.
> [LOW] (placement) Badge placement inline with the medication name (vs. in a right-hand margin, tested as an alternate) was preferred 5:1 — participants didn't want to look away from the text to check a flag.
> [LOW] (false positive tolerance) One deliberately-seeded false positive (a real but unusual drug name) was accepted by all 6 participants as "worth a second look, not annoying" — suggests some over-flagging is tolerable if the badge is visually light.
> Representative Quotes
> "That's different from the fuzzy-underline stuff — this one's telling me specifically 'check the drug name,' which is exactly what I'd want flagged." — Physician, Internal Medicine, P22
> "If it's not flagged, I might just... trust it more than I should. That's on me, but it's worth knowing the tool does that to people." — Nurse Practitioner, P24
> Recommendations
> Build the distinct medication-safety flag as its own variant, inline with the medication name, separate from generic confidence highlighting.
> Before rollout, pair the flag with brief onboarding copy or training-pilot messaging that explicitly counters the over-trust-on-unflagged-lines risk surfaced by 2 of 6 participants — don't let the flag imply "anything unflagged is guaranteed correct."
> Feed the false-positive tolerance finding to whoever tunes the sound-alike detection threshold — participants have headroom for a few extra false positives in exchange for not missing a real one.
> Follow-ups / Open Questions
> Needs a larger-N validation pass before this moves from concept test to build-ready spec.
> Over-trust-on-unflagged risk should be checked again once the post-GA monitoring dashboard (recommended 2026-01-13) has real error-rate data to compare against self-reported behavior.

**[2] Ambient Scribe — Post-GA Refinements** — Overview · `finding:ambient-scribe-post-ga-refinements`

> In a 6-person concept test, a distinct sound-alike-medication flag was recognized as different from generic confidence highlighting. **[3] Interview — Nurse Practitioner on Session Lock and Shared Exam-Room Workstations** · `raw:2026-04-28-interview-nurse-practitioner-session-lock-shared-workstations`

> Objective
> The 2026-02-17 contextual inquiry found that a session lock during active dictation freezes the ambient-scribe widget with no message, and that all 4 shadowed clinicians assumed the draft was lost and started dictating again, although the draft survives server-side (2026-02-17-session-lock-during-dictation/session-notes.md:38-41, read with correction-2026-09-27.md in that folder). A recovery flow with an explicit paused, draft-preserved state is designed but has not shipped. That session did not look at exam-room workstations shared by several clinicians. This session asked a primary care nurse practitioner, two months later, what a lock looks like in their visits and how a shared workstation affects it.
> Method
> Method: 1:1 interview, 45 min
> Researcher: Priya Patel
> Full participant roster: see participants.md in this folder
> Context: The participant is a nurse practitioner in an ambulatory primary care clinic, about five years in that role. They use the ambient scribe for most visits. Exam rooms in their clinic have a workstation that clinicians share across the day, signing in with a badge tap. The idle timeout is 10 minutes and is not adjustable per feature (2026-02-17-session-lock-during-dictation/session-notes.md:49-50). The paused state has not shipped, so a lock still freezes the widget with no message. They did not take part in any earlier session, including the 2026-02-17 inquiry. This is an interview about their own practice; nothing was shown or observed.
> Key Findings
> [HIGH] (lock during a visit) A lock happens to them when they step away from the workstation to the exam table within the room, typically for the physical exam, and the idle timeout fires while the scribe is listening. When they come back the widget looks frozen and shows no message. Their practice is to re-authenticate and scroll through the draft to see what is still there before they say anything more. They described learning this after re-dictating a visit summary early on and then finding the original draft intact; that is their account of their own practice, not an observed change. They put locks at "most afternoons, at least once", an informal impression.
> [HIGH] (shared workstations) Two or more clinicians use the same exam-room workstation on a clinic day, each signing in with a badge tap. They do not know what happens to their in-progress draft if a colleague signs in on that workstation while their session is locked, and they did not want to find out with a patient's visit. So they stop the scribe before leaving a room, even mid-visit, and start it again when they return. That avoids the question but splits one visit across several recordings, which they then have to read as one draft. How the product behaves in that case is not recorded in the corpus and was not settled here.
> [MEDIUM] (framing) They described the lock as one more sign-in in a day full of them, not as a safety behavior. That matches the framing the 2026-02-17 inquiry recorded ("one more annoying re-login", 2026-02-17-session-lock-during-dictation/session-notes.md:45-48); it is this participant's own description, and the earlier finding was not put to them.
> [MEDIUM] (what would help) Asked what they would want to see after a lock, without being shown anything, they said a message that the draft is saved and whose session it belongs to. That is an expectation; the paused state has not shipped and was not shown.
> Representative Quotes

> Add the shared exam-room workstation to the review of the paused-state recovery flow: what a clinician sees after a lock when a colleague has signed in on the same workstation.
> Make sure the paused-state message says whose draft is preserved, not only that a draft is.
> When the paused state ships, re-test whether clinicians still stop the scribe before leaving a shared room, as well as whether they still re-dictate.
> Follow-ups / Open Questions
> What happens to an in-progress draft when another clinician signs in on the same workstation during a lock is not recorded in the corpus and was not settled here.
> How often locks interrupt a visit is not measured; "most afternoons, at least once" is one nurse practitioner's impression.
